What five teams actually do with saved links

Fourteen weeks of saves from five product teams, and the same finding every time: the tool that stuck wrote into the place people already looked.

artifact.htmlsandboxed · scripts off

What we looked at

Five teams between eight and forty people, fourteen weeks of shelf activity, and one question: which saves came back? Not which links were stored — a shelf can hold a thousand and stay dead — but which ones were opened a second time, cited in a document, or found again by search.

The answer was consistent enough to be boring. Saves that came back were the ones somebody else could find: they had a name, a source, and a summary that said what the link was for. Saves that vanished were the ones that arrived as a bare URL with no note attached.

Three habits

  • File on the way in. A save that waits for a weekly tidy never gets one. The teams that kept a live shelf filed at the moment of saving, even badly.
  • Write the sentence you would say. The summary is not a abstract of the piece; it is the reason you sent it to someone. One sentence, in your own words, beats three generated paragraphs.
  • Cut, do not scroll. Nobody reads a list of four hundred. The useful shelf was always a filtered one — by folder, by label, by source — and the filter was the feature people described as "it knows what I meant".
A library is a promise that you will come back. Everything that makes the return cheaper is the product; everything else is storage.

What this means for the shelf

Two design consequences. The card has to carry enough of the save to be worth scanning — the summary, the source, the picture, the ref — because that is the surface where the decision to reopen happens. And the only navigation worth building is a cut of one list, not a second list beside it.

Neither is new. Both are the reason the shelf has one ref space, one search and one archive rather than a page per save.
